Summer Research Reading List

Research areas and readings for microlearning, adult ESOL, confidence, plateauing, transfer, and evaluation.

Summer Research Reading List

Purpose

This reading list supports two related projects:

  • redesigning the ESOL Class Companion app around Level 3 plateau learners
  • building evidence-backed microlearning experiences for adult professionals, including legal aid attorneys

The goal is to make summer design decisions with a stronger research base: not just "this feels better," but "this matches what we know about adult learning, cognitive load, language anxiety, retrieval, task-based learning, and transfer."

Core Design Question

How do we help busy adult learners move from knowledge to usable performance without wasting their time?

For ESOL students, that means helping Level 3 learners move through the plateau: less overthinking, more confidence, more usable language.

For legal aid attorneys, that means short, practical learning that transfers into client work, advocacy, supervision, documentation, court practice, and decision-making.

1. Level 3 Plateau, Anxiety, And Confidence

Why This Matters

Level 3 students are not beginners. They often know a lot, but that can create overthinking. They may know rules but hesitate to speak. Anxiety rises when they are aware of errors but do not yet have automatic control.

This should shape the app:

  • reduce choices
  • give one clear next step
  • practice language in meaningful tasks
  • repeat patterns until they feel usable
  • avoid long explanations that increase overthinking

Read

  • Foreign Language Classroom Anxiety - Horwitz, Horwitz, and Cope
  • Foreign and second language anxiety - Horwitz
  • Interlanguage, transfer and fossilization - Benjamins

Design Notes

  • Treat anxiety as a product problem, not just a classroom mood.
  • Make progress visible so students do not feel like they are regressing.
  • Use repeated low-stakes speaking and app practice.
  • Avoid making students choose from too many options when they are tired.

2. Task-Based Language Teaching And Speaking Practice

Why This Matters

The class is moving toward speaking, stations, role-plays, and real adult-life tasks. Task-Based Language Teaching is a strong research area for this direction.

The useful idea: language improves when learners use it to complete meaningful tasks, especially with planning, repetition, and feedback.

Read

  • Improving Speaking Fluency in a Task-Based Language Teaching Approach
  • Task repetition and L2 oral performance: A meta-analysis
  • Effectiveness of Task-Based Language Teaching in English Speaking Instruction: A Systematic Review

Design Notes

  • Pair each app module with a speaking task.
  • Let students repeat the same task with better language, not always start a new activity.
  • Build paths like: learn phrase -> practice choice -> play game -> say it to a partner.
  • For legal aid LMS: use task simulations, client interview decisions, issue spotting, letter drafting, and hearing prep instead of passive modules.

3. Focus On Form: The Balance Between Rules And Use

Why This Matters

The class should not abandon grammar rules. Rules give confidence. But grammar should not become isolated memorization.

Focus on form is the research frame for this balance: draw attention to grammar inside meaningful communication.

Read

  • Focus on form: A critical review - Rod Ellis
  • Focus on form overview

Design Notes

  • Keep grammar explanations short.
  • Put rules next to a real task.
  • Teach tenses through meaning and timeline use, not long isolated units.
  • Give corrective feedback in context.
  • For legal aid LMS: teach doctrine/procedure inside realistic case tasks, not as detached rule dumps.

4. Microlearning And Cognitive Load

Why This Matters

Working adults have limited time and limited cognitive bandwidth. Long modules, long guides, and too many choices increase friction.

Microlearning should not mean shallow learning. It should mean smaller units with a clear purpose, followed by retrieval, practice, and transfer.

Read

  • A systematic review of mobile-based microlearning in adult learner contexts
  • Cognitive Load Theory - InstructionalDesign.org
  • Cognitive load theory as an aid for instructional design - Cooper
  • Using Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning to create online multimedia lessons

Design Notes

  • Replace long grammar guides with 5-10 minute modules.
  • Remove unnecessary decisions from the student dashboard.
  • Use "Next Up" instead of category browsing as the main path.
  • Chunk learning into: learn, see, try, say/do, check.
  • For legal aid LMS: one micro-module should support one legal task, decision, or drafting move.

5. Retrieval Practice, Spacing, And Vocabulary

Why This Matters

Students need repeated retrieval, not just exposure. But the app data suggests optional daily habits are uneven. Required weekly vocab with accountability may work better than relying on optional daily review alone.

Read

  • Retrieval Practice Consistently Benefits Student Learning - Systematic Review
  • Effects of spacing on contextual vocabulary learning
  • The effectiveness of L2 vocabulary instruction: a meta-analysis

Design Notes

  • Weekly quizzes are not just accountability; they are retrieval practice.
  • Vocab should be used in class, in homework, and in speaking tasks.
  • Six words per week may support deeper retrieval better than a large list.
  • Daily Vocab Review can remain useful, but the required path should not depend on optional usage.
  • For legal aid LMS: use short retrieval checks after realistic scenarios, then revisit the same concept later in a new case context.

6. Mobile-Assisted Learning And Gamification

Why This Matters

The LMS already changed outside-of-class behavior. Students are doing more app work than they did with Google Classroom. The next step is to make the app more directed, not necessarily more game-like.

Gamification should support meaningful progress, not distract from learning.

Read

  • Mobile-assisted and gamification-based language learning: a systematic literature review
  • A Meta-Analysis on Mobile-Assisted Language Learning Applications
  • A systematic review of collaborative mobile-assisted language learning
  • Mobile-Assisted English Vocabulary Learning: A Systematic Review

Design Notes

  • Use game maps carefully: they should clarify the path, not create more choices.
  • Prioritize personal milestones over class competition.
  • Show lifetime completions, weekly streaks, quiz completion history, and progress through paths.
  • For legal aid LMS: gamification should probably look like progress, readiness, and mastery, not playful competition.

7. Adult Learning And Professional Learning

Why This Matters

Both ESOL students and legal aid attorneys are adult learners with real responsibilities. Adult learners need relevance, respect, autonomy, and immediate usefulness.

Knowles' andragogy is useful, but use it critically. Adults still benefit from structure, guidance, and accountability. "Self-directed" does not mean "make them figure everything out alone."

Read

  • Andragogy in Practice: Applying a Theoretical Framework
  • Knowles' Adult Learning Theory overview
  • Adult learning theory reference PDF - ERIC

Design Notes

  • Respect adults by making the work meaningful.
  • Give clear assignments and due dates when accountability helps.
  • Explain why the task matters.
  • Avoid infantilizing language, badges, or rewards.
  • For legal aid LMS: adults need training that maps directly to legal judgment, ethical risk, client outcomes, and practice confidence.

8. Multimedia Learning And Interface Design

Why This Matters

The app is not only curriculum. It is an interface. Interface choices affect learning because they affect attention, cognitive load, and confidence.

Read

  • Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning - Cambridge Handbook chapter
  • Using multimedia learning theory for online lessons
  • Mayer multimedia learning research meta-analysis

Design Notes

  • Do not overload screens.
  • Keep the primary action obvious.
  • Use visuals to support meaning, not decorate.
  • Reduce split attention.
  • Put examples close to explanations.
  • For legal aid LMS: diagrams, timelines, issue maps, and decision trees can support reasoning if they are clean and task-linked.

Week 6: Evaluation Plan

Read:

  • any skipped sources above
  • your own app data

Questions:

  • What will prove the redesign works?
  • What data should be collected weekly?
  • What student feedback questions should be asked?

Evaluation Ideas

Use simple classroom-friendly evaluation. The goal is not a formal dissertation; the goal is better decisions.

Quantitative

  • weekly app completion rate
  • assignment completion by type
  • quiz scores and retakes
  • number of students completing weekly vocab
  • number of students completing Daily Vocab Review
  • time between assignment and completion
  • guide start vs completion rate
  • game start vs completion rate

Qualitative

Ask students:

  • Did you know what to do next?
  • Did the homework feel useful?
  • Was anything a waste of time?
  • Did the app help you feel more prepared for class?
  • Did the app help you speak more confidently?
  • Which activity helped you most this week?

Teacher Reflection

Track:

  • Did homework show up in class discussion?
  • Did students use the weekly vocabulary while speaking?
  • Did quizzes reveal real gaps?
  • Did the app reduce or increase teacher workload?
  • Did the path feel respectful of students' time?

Working Research-Backed Principles

  1. Adults need relevance and respect.
  2. Working memory is limited; reduce unnecessary choices.
  3. Microlearning works only if it includes practice, retrieval, and transfer.
  4. Grammar rules help when they support communication.
  5. Repetition is not childish when the task is meaningful.
  6. Speaking confidence grows through low-stakes repeated performance.
  7. Quizzes can be learning tools, not just assessment.
  8. Optional practice helps some students, but required paths create broader uptake.
  9. Gamification should show personal progress, not just competition.
  10. The app should reduce anxiety by making the next step obvious.

    11. Questions To Keep Open

    • What is the right balance between teacher-curated assignments and learner autonomy?
    • How short is short enough for a micro-module?
    • What should be required homework vs optional review?
    • How can the app encourage speaking without becoming awkward or surveillance-heavy?
    • What does adult-appropriate gamification look like?
    • How can legal-aid microlearning measure real transfer into practice?
    • How can the ESOL app measure confidence without over-testing students?
