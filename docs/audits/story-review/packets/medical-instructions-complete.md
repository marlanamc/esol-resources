## 2. Clinic signs & prescription labels

> 🖼 **Scene:** Walking in the front door  
> _Photo shows: Migration health assessment centre sign on wall._

Imperatives: the language of signs

Every sign, sticker, and prescription label you pass in a clinic is an imperative: a short command with no subject. The subject "you" is understood.

Formula

[Base verb] + (object/details)

The subject "you" is invisible.

Real prescription sticker

Notice — every line starts with a verb: Take, Finish, Do not, Avoid, Call. No "you". That's the whole trick.

Three flavors of imperative

1. Direct command (strong)

"Stop smoking." · "Take this twice a day."

2. Polite request (+ please)

"Please fill out this form." · "Please wait here."

3. Negative (Don't / Do not)

"Don't eat before the test." · "Do not take on empty stomach."

## 3. Dr. Chen's office: declaratives

> _Photo shows: A doctor in a white coat explaining something to a seated patient._

Declaratives: the language of conversations

When your doctor explains what's happening, she uses declaratives: full sentences with a subject. They feel softer and more human than imperatives — perfect for talking.

Formula

Subject + Verb + (object/details)

"You…", "I…", "We…", "The medicine…"

Listen in on a real appointment

- **Dr. Chen:** "I think you have a sinus infection. You need to take antibiotics for ten days."
- **Rosa (patient):** "I have two small children at home. Can I still pick them up?"
- **Dr. Chen:** "You can lift them, but you should rest when they nap. You shouldn't drive if you feel dizzy."
- **Rosa (patient):** "I'll take the medicine with breakfast. Do I need another appointment?"
- **Dr. Chen:** "You don't need to come back unless the fever returns. You're allowed to call the nurse line any time."
- **Rosa (patient):** "Thank you. I'll call if anything changes."

Common declarative patterns in healthcare

## 4. Same idea, five tones

The tone ladder

English has many ways to say "I want you to take this medicine." The difference is tone — how strong or soft it feels. Climb the ladder from urgent at the top to gentle at the bottom.

Same day, four different speakers

- **Sign on door:** "Wash your hands before entering." (bare imperative)
- **Nurse:** "You need to wash your hands before entering." (firm declarative)
- **Dr. Chen:** "You should always wash your hands before seeing the baby." (advice)
- **Pharmacist:** "You could use this hand sanitizer too, if you prefer." (suggestion)

What is a modal?

A modal is a small helper verb (should, must, can, may, need to) that changes the meaning of the main verb. It sits between the subject and the base verb — and never changes form.

Modal formula

Subject + Modal + Base Verb

You should rest. · You must finish. · I can ask.

## 6. The pharmacist's warning: should vs. must

> 🖼 **Scene:** At the pharmacy counter  
> _Photo shows: A pharmacist handing a prescription bag across a pharmacy counter._

- **Pharmacist:** "Before I hand this over — a few things. You should take it with food. You must finish the full bottle. And you must not drink alcohol while taking it."
- **Patient:** "What about coffee?"
- **Pharmacist:** "Coffee is fine. You can have your normal cup."

Should = friendly advice

Should means this is a good idea, but you have a choice.

You should drink plenty of water.

You should take this with food.

You shouldn't skip any doses.

Must / Must not = safety warning

Must means required. Must not means dangerous or forbidden.

You must finish all the antibiotics.

You must not drink alcohol with this medication.

You must not drive after taking this.

## 7. Reception desk: can, may, need to, allowed to

> 🖼 **Scene:** Front desk — check-in window  
> _Photo shows: A patient checking in at a clinic reception desk._

Your rights in a clinic

You're not just a patient — you have rights. Four patterns help you use them out loud:

CAN I…? — informal permission

"Can I ask a quick question?" · "Can I bring my husband?"

MAY I…? — formal / respectful

"May I have a copy of my records?" · "May I request a translator?"

I'M ALLOWED TO… — your right

"I'm allowed to see my test results." · "I'm allowed to request a second opinion."

NEED TO — everyday necessity

"I need to speak with a nurse." · "Do I need to fast before the test?"

Rosa's second visit

## 9. Back to work: calling out sick

> 🖼 **Scene:** Kitchen table, 7:02 AM  
> _Photo shows: Woman reading document at kitchen table with coffee_

Same grammar, different power dynamic

Your boss can use imperatives with you ("Send the report by noon"). You usually shouldn't do the same back — it sounds like an order. Declaratives and polite questions are your safer tools.

Two ways to call in sick

❌ Too direct

"Give me the day off. I'm sick."

Bare imperative to your manager = sounds like a demand.

✅ Respectful

"Hi Maria — I'm not feeling well and I'd like to take a sick day. Could I join the meeting tomorrow instead?"

Declarative + polite question = professional.

Monday morning call

- **Manager:** "Good morning, Luis. What's up?"
- **Luis:** "Hi Maria. I'm really sorry — I have a bad fever. I think I need to stay home today."
- **Manager:** "Okay. Please rest. You don't need to join the standup."
- **Luis:** "Thank you. Could I submit the report tomorrow instead?"
- **Manager:** "Sure — you can. Feel better."

Employee-to-boss toolkit

"I'd like to request time off next Friday."

"Could I have a little more time to finish this?"

"Would you mind reviewing my draft when you're free?"

"I will send the updated file by 5 PM."

"I'm not sure I understand — could you explain again?"
