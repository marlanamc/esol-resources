## 1. You just walked into the ER

> 🖼 **Scene:** The ER hallway, Friday, 8:15 AM  
> _Photo shows: A bright, well-lit hospital hallway with doors and clear overhead lighting._

Luis is a line cook at a seafood restaurant in East Boston. On Friday he wakes up at 5 AM with a fever of 103. His throat is on fire. His dinner shift starts at 3 PM, and Friday is the busiest night of the week. His wife, Marta, stays home with their four-year-old, and Luis takes a cab to the ER.

At 6 AM he texts his manager, Angela: "Sick. Going to the ER. I'll call you later." She writes back: "Ok. Call me when you know more. I'll need a doctor's note."

Luis: "I have a high fever, my throat really hurts, and I can't keep food down."

Nurse: "Please sit down. You should fill out this form. You must wear this wristband the whole time you are here."

The nurse used three kinds of sentences in ten seconds. Luis will hear them and use them all day: on signs, at the pharmacy, on the phone with Angela, and on his discharge sheet.

Three kinds of sentences

🗯️ Imperatives: a command

"Take this medicine." · "Don't drive after."

💬 Declaratives: a statement

"You need to take this." · "I recommend resting."

🔑 Modals: small words inside declaratives that change the meaning

should (advice) · must (required) · can (permission) · need to (necessity)

## 2. Clinic signs & prescription labels

> 🖼 **Scene:** A sign on the hospital wall. Luis waits 40 minutes and reads every sign twice.  
> _Photo shows: Migration health assessment centre sign on wall._

Imperatives: the language of signs

In the waiting room, Luis has nothing to do but read the walls: Please take a number. Cover your cough. Tell the nurse if you feel worse. Almost every sign, sticker, and prescription label in a hospital is an imperative: a short command with no subject. The subject "you" is understood.

Formula

[Base verb] + (object/details)

The subject "you" is invisible.

The sticker on Luis's bottle

Later today, Luis's antibiotic will come with this label. It talks the same way the signs do.

Every line starts with a verb: Take, Finish, Do not, Keep, Call. There is no "you".

Three flavors of imperative

1. Direct command (strong)

"Stop smoking." · "Take this twice a day."

2. Polite request (+ please)

"Please fill out this form." · "Please wait here."

3. Negative (Don't / Do not)

"Don't eat before the test." · "Do not take on empty stomach."

## 3. Dr. Chen's office: declaratives

> 🖼 **Scene:** Exam room 3, 9:20 AM. Dr. Chen has Luis's test result.  
> _Photo shows: A doctor in a white coat explaining something to a seated patient._

Declaratives: the language of conversations

When Dr. Chen explains what's happening, she uses declaratives: full sentences with a subject. They sound softer than imperatives, so people use them when they talk face to face.

Formula

Subject + Verb + (object/details)

"You…", "I…", "We…", "The medicine…"

The test result

- **Dr. Chen:** "You have strep throat. You need to take an antibiotic for ten days."
- **Luis:** "I'm a line cook, and my shift starts at three. Can I work tonight?"
- **Dr. Chen:** "No. You can't work with food until you've taken the antibiotic for 24 hours and the fever is gone."
- **Luis:** "I have a four-year-old daughter at home. Can I still hug her?"
- **Dr. Chen:** "You can hug her, but you shouldn't share cups or forks. You should wash your hands a lot."
- **Luis:** "Okay. I'll take it with breakfast and dinner. I'll call Angela when I get home."

Common declarative patterns in healthcare

## 4. Same idea, five tones

The tone ladder

English has many ways to say "I want you to take this medicine." The difference is tone: how strong or soft it feels. Climb the ladder from urgent at the top to gentle at the bottom.

Five ways to say "wash your hands"

Luis sees the same idea four times on Friday, and once more every day at work. Each one sits on a different rung.

- **ER door sign:** "Wash your hands before entering." (bare imperative)
- **Nurse:** "You need to wash your hands before you go back to your bed." (firm declarative)
- **Dr. Chen:** "You should wash your hands before you pick up your daughter." (advice)
- **Pharmacist:** "You could keep some hand sanitizer by the door, too." (suggestion)

## 5. Modals crash course

> 🖼 **Scene:** Dr. Chen's desk. She writes Luis's prescription.

Look back at what Dr. Chen told Luis: you need to take, you can't work, you shouldn't share. The small word before the verb decides everything. Can Luis go to work? Does he have a choice?

What is a modal?

A modal is a small helper verb (should, must, can, may, need to) that changes the meaning of the main verb. It sits between the subject and the base verb, and it never changes form.

Modal formula

Subject + Modal + Base Verb

You should rest. · You must finish. · I can ask.

## 6. The pharmacist's warning: should vs. must

> 🖼 **Scene:** The hospital pharmacy, 11:00 AM. Luis's antibiotic is ready, and so is Marta's allergy refill.  
> _Photo shows: A pharmacist handing a prescription bag across a pharmacy counter._

- **Pharmacist:** "A few things before you go. You should take it with food, and you must finish the full bottle."
- **Luis:** "Even if I feel better on Monday?"
- **Pharmacist:** "Even then. And you must not drink alcohol while you're taking it."
- **Luis:** "No beer. Okay. What about coffee?"
- **Pharmacist:** "Coffee is fine. You can have your normal cup."

Should = friendly advice

Should means this is a good idea, but you have a choice.

You should drink plenty of water.

You should take this with food.

You shouldn't skip any doses.

Must / Must not = safety warning

Must means required. Must not means dangerous or forbidden.

You must finish all the antibiotics.

You must not drink alcohol with this medication.

You must not drive after taking this.

Side by side

## 7. Reception desk: can, may, need to, allowed to

> 🖼 **Scene:** The ER front desk, 11:20 AM. Luis is halfway to the door when he remembers Angela's text.  
> _Photo shows: A patient checking in at a clinic reception desk._

One more thing before he leaves

Angela needs a doctor's note, and Luis wants instructions he can read at home. Four patterns help him ask:

CAN I…? Informal permission

"Can I ask a quick question?" · "Can I call my wife?"

MAY I…? Formal, respectful

"May I have a copy of my records?" · "May I have a note for work?"

I'M ALLOWED TO… Your right

"I'm allowed to see my test results." · "I'm allowed to request a second opinion."

NEED TO: everyday necessity

"I need to speak with a nurse." · "Do I need to sign this?"

The work note

- **Luis:** "Excuse me. May I have a note for work? My manager needs one."
- **Nicole:** "Sure. You need to show a photo ID first."
- **Luis:** "Here's my license. Can I also get my discharge instructions in Spanish?"
- **Nicole:** "Yes, I'll print them. And you're allowed to ask for a free interpreter if anything isn't clear."
- **Luis:** "Thanks. Do I need to sign anything?"
- **Nicole:** "Just this form. Then you can go home and sleep."

## 9. Back to work: calling out sick

> 🖼 **Scene:** Home, 12:40 PM. Marta reads the discharge sheet at the kitchen table while Luis calls work.  
> _Photo shows: Woman reading document at kitchen table with coffee_

Luis gets home at 12:30. His shift starts in two and a half hours. Now he has to make the call he promised Angela at 6 AM.

Same grammar, different power

Angela can use imperatives with Luis ("Prep the fish by four"). Luis usually shouldn't do the same back. It sounds like an order. Declaratives and polite questions are safer.

Two ways to call out sick

❌ Too direct

"Give me the night off. I'm sick."

A bare imperative to your manager sounds like a demand.

✅ Respectful

"Hi Angela. I'm not feeling well, and I need to take tonight off. Could Kevin cover my shift?"

Declarative + polite question = professional.

The call

- **Angela:** "Hi, Luis. How are you feeling? What did the doctor say?"
- **Luis:** "I'm really sorry. I have strep throat. I can't work with food until Sunday."
- **Angela:** "Oh no. Okay, please rest. You don't need to worry about the weekend."
- **Luis:** "Thank you. Could Kevin cover my shift tonight? He asked for extra hours this week."
- **Angela:** "Good idea. I'll call him. Can you text me a photo of the doctor's note?"
- **Luis:** "Sure. I'll send it right now."

Employee-to-boss toolkit

"I'd like to request next Thursday off."

"Could I have ten more minutes to finish the prep?"

"Would you mind showing me the new menu again?"

"I will send the doctor's note by 5 PM."

"I'm not sure I understand. Could you explain the new schedule again?"
