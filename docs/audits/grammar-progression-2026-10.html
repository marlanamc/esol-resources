Level 3 ESOL · FY27 · Audit of October 17, 2026

Grammar Progression, Weeks 1–36

The unit topics stay fixed. This audit looks only at the order of the grammar: what each week teaches, whether it builds on what came before, and where students might get overwhelmed or meet something too late. The changes cover Weeks 7–36; the overview at the bottom shows the whole year, starting with Week 1.

Drafted on branch grammar-progression-fixes Tests and audits pass Not merged yet Database sync still needed
The short answer

The sequence works. A few weeks needed fixing.

Most strands come back several times with gaps in between, and each hard point builds on something taught earlier. Past perfect (W20) comes right after past continuous (W19). Reported speech (W30) comes after past perfect. The third conditional (W31) comes after both. May is mostly review, which is a good way to end the year.

Weeks 1–6 are the foundation: tense review, parts of speech, the five verb forms, questions, and present simple routines. Every later strand builds on them, and they needed no changes.

The problems were local: the first conditional sitting in an optional slot, one week with two required guides, a "how long" lesson taught three times, future forms with no guide of their own, and a year-end review that treated three untaught tenses as review.

What changed

Seven weeks edited

W7

Our Voice, Our Vote

Before
Let's Make a Suggestion was required. Zero + First Conditional was an optional extra.
After
Zero + First Conditional is required. Let's Make a Suggestion is the optional extra. Still one required guide.

Why: The first conditional is core grammar that the second conditional (W22) and all four conditionals (W34) build on, so it shouldn't be optional. It also keeps the election link: the guide ends with "A Ride to the Polls," the week before Election Day on November 3. Suggestions still come back in W8 ("should" for advice) and W10 ("Suggestions for the City"). I also fixed six exercises where the answer could be copied from the teaching text.

W9

Helping + Volunteering

Before
Two required guides: Have You Ever and Just, Already, Yet.
After
Have You Ever is required. Just, Already, Yet is an optional Challenge.

Why: W9 is students' first time with the present perfect. Learning the form and two different uses in one week is a lot. Now they learn one idea first, and just/already/yet comes back as a required lesson in W24.

W14

Understanding the News for Econ

Before
Three reading-skills pages (headlines, charts, jobs). No grammar point.
After
A fourth page: "What will happen next? Will and going to." Three news headlines show a prediction (will), a decided plan (going to), and a real possible future (if + present, will + verb), with a "no will after if" warning.

Why: Future forms never had their own guide. "Going to" only appeared in dialogues, yet the final week reviewed "all four future tenses." News headlines are full of predictions, so this week was the natural slot. The if + will box also gives students a second look at the first conditional from W7 before the second conditional in W22.

W15

Fall Review + Class Party

Before
All Verb Tenses Overview: all 12 English tenses on one timeline, including past perfect continuous and future perfect continuous.
After
A new guide, "Fall Review: Six Tenses You Know," a sequel to the W1 bus-stop review. One dialogue, then a page for each pair: usually vs. this week, Rosa's Saturday timeline, her "since September" checklist, and her plans vs. guesses for the break. The 12-tense overview is an optional Challenge.

Why: By December, students have practiced six tenses. Showing all twelve in a relaxed party week puts past perfect and the perfect continuous tenses in front of them months before they're taught (W20, W25). The 12-tense overview still lives in the grammar library, and W36 links to it.

W24

Job Applications + Interviews

Before
"Present Perfect + How Long": How long have you…?, for vs. since, the form, still going vs. finished, interview.
After
"Just, Already, Yet: The Job Search": Rosa calls HR (already), her inbox shows a new email (just), her job-search checklist (yet), still going vs. finished (kept), and the interview (retuned).

Why: W11 already teaches how long + for/since with nearly the same pages, and W25 opens with "How long have you been working here?" That made three "how long" lessons. Job searching is full of just/already/yet ("I've already applied," "I haven't heard back yet"), so the week now teaches something new while still practicing the present perfect. The link, file and photos are unchanged.

W31

Third Conditional + Pharmacy

Before
Page 1 was titled "I Should Have Called Sooner." Its unscramble was a mixed conditional: "If I had taken the medicine, I would feel better now." The last page had no type-in exercise.
After
Page 1 is "If I Had Come Sooner," and its unscramble is a plain third conditional. The last page has a type-in item.

Why: April already has three hard structures in a row (stop taking / stop to take, reported speech, third conditional). The first page of a new structure should teach only that structure. "Should have" and mixed conditionals are separate grammar points.

W36

All the Tenses: A Year in Review

Before
"One Year, Eleven Tenses." The last page reviewed future simple, future continuous, future perfect and future perfect continuous, with type-in questions.
After
"One Year, One Timeline" shows only tenses practiced this year. The last page is "Will, Going To, and a Sneak Peek." The three advanced future tenses sit in a labeled Level 4 preview box with read-only questions.

Why: Students only saw those three tenses once, on the December tense chart, and never practiced them. A review shouldn't ask them to produce something they were never taught. They still get to see what's coming next year.

Weekly quizzes

Quizzes only test what the guides teach

Each weekly quiz has verb forms, two hand-written "apply" sentences, and two grammar questions pulled from that week's first required guide.

  • W7 now asks about the first conditional. Its apply sentences became "If you ___ a letter to the council, they will read it" and "If I ___ an email to the mayor, her office will answer me."
  • W24 now asks about just and yet. Its apply sentence used "since," so it became "I have already ___ the safety rules."
  • W36 asks about going to, which is now taught in W14 and W36.
  • W9, W14, W31: I checked the apply sentences, and they still match their guides. W14 has no quiz.

The quiz file was already out of date

Before any of this work, 13 weeks of quizzes (W11, W12, W13, W17, W19, W25, W27, W28, W29, W32, W33, W34) no longer matched edits made to their guides, and nothing checked for it. The rebuild brings every quiz back in line. A new check in health:gate now fails CI if a guide changes without a quiz rebuild.

Left as is

Watch these, but no change for now

  • Modals take up five guides (W7, W8, W17, W21, W28), and W21 mostly repeats W8 and W17. Revisiting a strand is fine. If you ever want that week for something new, relative clauses (who / that / which) are the biggest gap, and they suit the workforce unit: "I'm someone who…", "a job that…". That swap means a new guide and a week-title change, which also requires a vocab reseed.
  • April is the densest month: W29, W30 and W31 are three of the hardest items of the year. The catch-up time in W30 helps. Check those quiz scores.
  • W32 used to → W33 be used to puts a classic confusing pair back to back. W33's "Don't Mix Them Up" page is built for that contrast. Check how the W33 quiz scores come out.
