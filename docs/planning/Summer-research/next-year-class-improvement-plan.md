# Next Year Class Improvement Plan

## Purpose

This is a summer planning document for improving next year's ESOL class. The main goal is to make the class feel more useful, adult, and community-centered while still building strong English foundations.

Students should leave each week feeling like they learned something real about life, work, school, health, housing, money, or their rights. Grammar and vocabulary should support that goal instead of becoming the whole point of the week.

The class should also become much more speaking-centered. Students need more conversations, more partner practice, more role-plays, more stations, and fewer worksheets.

## Core Principle

This is a community school first. English matters because it helps students handle adult life:

- calling health insurance to check a prescription price
- filing a complaint with the Department of Labor
- understanding a workplace notice
- asking a pharmacy or clinic the right questions
- reading a lease, bill, letter, form, or school message
- explaining a problem clearly and respectfully
- knowing what agency, office, or person to contact

Adult-skill topics to build into lessons, app references, warm-ups, or stations:

- Tylenol vs. Advil
- medicine names + pharmacist questions
- healthy cooking oils
- fiber + carbohydrates + blood sugar
- medicine labels, insurance cards, dosage charts, nutrition labels
- health claims online: what evidence is missing?

Boundary: the teacher does not need to be the expert in every topic. The class teaches students how to ask better questions, compare information, read labels, and make safer decisions.

The best weeks should feel like: "I learned English, and I learned how to do something useful."

The class should also protect students' time. Every quiz, test, homework assignment, app activity, and worksheet should have real meaning behind it. Nothing should be assigned just to assign something. If students are asked to do work, it should truly matter.

## 1. Rethink The Grammar Guides

The medical grammar guides feel like the right model because they are not only grammar-heavy. They teach practical real-world topics at the same time.

At the same time, many grammar guides are too long. They can feel like 15-page grammar packets with long mini-quizzes attached. Next year, the goal should be shorter microlearning modules that students can finish, remember, and revisit.

### Direction

Grammar guides should become topic-based learning journeys, not grammar explanations with a few examples added. But those journeys do not need to live on one long page.

Instead of one giant guide, consider a learning path made of smaller modules:

- short concept lesson
- real-world example
- quick practice
- speaking task
- timeline check when relevant
- short quiz or exit check

Each guide should answer:

- What real situation is this grammar helping students handle?
- What adult-life topic are students learning?
- What documents, calls, conversations, or decisions might they actually face?
- What should students be able to do by the end?

### Microlearning Direction

Each module should be small enough that students can complete it without losing the thread.

Possible module structure:

| Part | Goal |
|---|---|
| Learn | one clear idea |
| See it | one real-world example |
| Try it | 3-5 quick practice questions |
| Say it | one partner/speaking task |
| Check it | short exit question or mini-quiz |

Long mini-quizzes should be reconsidered. Quizzes can still matter, but they may work better as short checks inside a path instead of one large quiz at the end of a long guide.

## 2. Make Every Week Teach Real Adult Knowledge

Each week should have a practical topic students can explain or use by the end of class.

This does not mean every class becomes a civics lesson. It means the reading, listening, speaking, grammar, and vocabulary should point toward something real.

Speaking should be the main way students process that real-world topic. If the topic is health insurance, they should practice the phone call. ## 3. Make Speaking The Center Of Class

The class should revolve around speaking. Worksheets should be limited and purposeful. More class time should go to conversations, stations, speaking games, role-plays, interviews, problem-solving tasks, and repeated practice with useful language.

### Direction

The default question for planning should be:

> How will students say this out loud many times?

Students should not only understand the grammar or vocabulary. They should be able to use it in conversation with another person.

### Tuesday Stations

Tuesdays should usually be station days.

Stations can include:

- speaking games
- role-play cards
- partner interviews
- vocabulary conversation practice
- timeline tense sorting or sentence-building
- grammar stations with short focused tasks
- teacher-led correction or mini-conference station

The grammar can still be present, but it should live inside speaking and interaction whenever possible.

### Worksheet Rule

Use very few worksheets.

The regular printed worksheet should be the weekly warm-up from the worksheet folder. Aside from small station materials, quiz prep, cards, or special printouts, the warm-up should be the main thing that needs to be printed for class.

Worksheets should only be used when they clearly support the speaking task, give students a record to keep, help prepare for a quiz, or organize the weekly plan. They should not become the main class activity.

### Weekly Warm-Up System

The warm-up becomes the printed anchor for the week. It is not just filler at the beginning of class. It should carry the vocabulary, grammar, objectives, homework, and quiz accountability in one predictable format.

#### Tuesday Warm-Up

The Tuesday warm-up should introduce the week.

Front:

- 6 new vocabulary words in context
- short grammar practice connected to the week's real-world topic
- possibly a quick `-ed` or verb noticing task

Back:

- weekly class plan
- student objectives for the week
- homework for the week with due dates
- quiz topics for the week

The back matters because students should know exactly what the week is about and what they are responsible for. This supports adult accountability without infantilizing them.

#### Thursday Warm-Up

The Thursday warm-up can be two pages because it is doing heavier review and transfer work.

It should include:

- review and retrieval of Tuesday's 6 vocabulary words
- 3 verbs of the week
- `-ed` ending practice when relevant
- review of the grammar topic
- timeline tense practice when relevant
- one real-world literacy task, such as evaluating a chart, insurance card, medicine label, bill, newspaper article, public notice, or data claim
- one short practical writing task

Thursday should feel like students are pulling the week together before the quiz or checkpoint.

## 4. Explore A Different Way To Teach Tenses

Students are not coming into class fresh. Most have already had at least two years of present simple, past simple, continuous, and some future. Next year should not spend a full month on present simple alone as if students have never seen it.

Tenses should be worked into the lessons more naturally and revisited constantly through real situations, speaking, timelines, and weekly practice.

### Direction

Move away from long isolated tense units.

Instead of:

> This month is present simple. Next month is past simple.

Try:

> This week we are solving a real-life problem, and we will use the tenses we need to talk about habits, past experiences, current problems, and future plans.

Rules still matter. They give students confidence, language to explain what they are doing, and structure when English feels chaotic. But the rule should support communication, not become the whole lesson.

### Balance To Aim For

| Too Much Rules | Better Balance | Too Little Structure |
|---|---|---|
| memorizing forms without using them | clear rule + real use + speaking practice | only conversation with no correction |
| one tense isolated for weeks | tenses compared and revisited often | students guessing with no pattern |
| worksheets first | timeline, meaning, sentence, conversation | no explicit grammar language |

### Teaching Questions

When planning tense instruction, ask:

- What does this tense help students say in real life?
- Have they already learned this before?
- What actually needs review: form, meaning, pronunciation, question form, negative form, or word order?
- Can this be taught through comparison instead of isolation?
- Can students see it on a timeline?
- Can they use it in a conversation today?

### Summer Work

- [ ] Review the yearly tense sequence and identify places where tense units are too isolated.
- [ ] Decide which tenses need full lessons and which need spiral review.
- [ ] Build mixed-tense weekly practice instead of single-tense blocks.
- [ ] Create tense comparison routines using timelines.
- [ ] Keep short rule explanations, but pair every rule with speaking practice.
- [ ] Look for ways to teach present simple, past simple, continuous, future, and present perfect together through real topics.

## 5. Use Guided Pattern Discovery

Level 3 students often know rules but hesitate to speak. They need to notice patterns in real language before long explanations or long drills. Guided discovery gives them a reason for the rule and reduces overthinking.

This is not isolated drilling. Discovery comes first. Short practice and speaking come after.

### Example

Show students real sentences like:

- I plan to move closer to work.
- We plan to call the clinic tomorrow.
- They decided to change jobs.
- She wants to ask about the rent.
- He needs to bring his insurance card.

Then ask: What comes after verbs like `plan`, `decide`, `want`, and `need`?

Students discover: **there is `to` before the next verb.**

Name it lightly: these verbs take **to + verb** (an infinitive). One clear line — not a long lecture.

### The Sequence

1. **Notice** — show 6–8 real sentences about a topic (job change, housing, health, school).
2. **Discover** — ask what is the same after certain verbs or in certain structures.
3. **Name lightly** — one clear rule line, not a long lecture.
4. **Try** — a few low-stakes choices (`I plan to move` vs `I plan moving`).
5. **Use** — a speaking task that requires the pattern immediately.

Chunk it like: learn → notice → try → say → check.

### Good Drilling vs Weak Drilling

| Good drilling | Weak drilling |
|---|---|
| 5–10 items, one pattern, after discovery | 30 blanks before students know why |
| tied to a real topic | random verb lists with no context |
| mixed with speaking reuse | worksheets only, no conversation |
| "Which sounds better?" comparisons | same mistake corrected with no explanation |

### First Pattern To Build: Verb + Infinitive

Start narrow and high-frequency before the full gerund vs infinitive unit:

- **verbs:** plan, decide, want, need, hope, try
- **frame:** subject + verb + to + verb
- **topics:** summer plans, job decisions, housing choices, calling a clinic, school meetings

**Sample discovery text:** someone talking about moving, changing jobs, or calling the doctor. Highlight `I plan to…`, `We decided to…`, `She wants to…`, `He needs to…`.

**Optional contrast seed:** `I enjoy working` vs `I plan to work` — plants gerunds later without opening the whole -ing vs to system at once.

### Where It Lives In Class And App

- **Tuesday:** short notice activity before the grammar station
- **Thursday:** retrieval through speaking ("What do you plan to do this summer?")
- **App:** weekly "notice the pattern" micro task before full guide content
- **Spiral review:** revisit in workplace, housing, and advocacy units — not one isolated grammar month

## 6. Start Present Perfect Earlier

**Decision (2026):** Present perfect intro moves to **September Unit 1, Week 4**. Verb forms overview (v1, v1-3rd, v1-ing, v2, v3) starts **Week 3** so weekly verb quizzes have a shared language from the beginning of the year.

Present perfect should start near the beginning of the year instead of waiting until later. Students need repeated exposure over time because it connects to so many adult-life situations. Level 2 teachers report students requesting present perfect by end of year — September intro meets them where they are ready.

### Why

Present perfect shows up in:

- job interviews: "Have you worked in a kitchen before?"
- medical forms: "Have you had surgery?"
- housing: "Have you ever been evicted?"
- school meetings: "Has your child received services before?"
- workplace problems: "I have asked my manager three times."

### Direction

Introduce present perfect early in a practical way. Do not wait until students are "ready" for the whole grammar explanation. Start with useful sentence frames and build from there.

September Week 4 = short intro only. October+ = spiral in community contexts. **March Unit 7, Weeks 2–3** = in-depth block where **present perfect and present perfect continuous are taught together** (career stories, work experience).

### Present Perfect + Present Perfect Continuous (Paired — March)

When present perfect gets real class time, teach **perfect and perfect continuous together** — not PPC alone weeks later.

| Form | Example frame | Career context |
|---|---|---|
| Present perfect | "I have worked at three restaurants." | Completed experience, life history |
| Present perfect continuous | "I have been working there for two years." | Duration, ongoing situation |

This pairing worked well in practice: students see both forms in the same topic (work history) instead of learning PPC in isolation.

### Homework Principles

- Keep assignments short enough to be realistic.
- Make homework directly connected to the week.
- Assign specific tasks, not vague optional practice.
- Use due dates.

## 12. Practice -ed Endings Every Day

Every class day should include practice with `-ed` endings. Yes, every day.

This may be one of the biggest struggles in teaching English. Students need to hear the endings, say them, repeat them, and build the instinct until they just know what sounds right.

### Direction

Make `-ed` endings a daily pronunciation habit, not an occasional phonics lesson.

**September Week 3:** brief intro to the three sounds (**-t, -d, -id**) on a reusable warm-up chart. After that, spiral every week with the 3 verbs of the week — not a separate phonics unit each month.

The routine can stay very small. It does not need to take over class. The key is repetition over time.

The usual "voiced vs. unvoiced" explanation may not be the best teaching method for this class. It is linguistically accurate, but it can be too abstract and confusing. Summer planning should focus on finding a more teachable approach that students can hear, feel, and use without needing technical vocabulary.

### The Three Sounds

Repeat the three `-ed` endings over and over:

| Sound | Example | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| /t/ | worked, helped, asked | after voiceless sounds |
| /d/ | called, played, cleaned | after voiced sounds |
| /id/ | wanted, needed, decided | after /t/ or /d/ |

The chart is useful for teacher planning, but it may not be the first explanation students need.

### Teaching Approach To Explore

Try building the routine around sound groups, listening, and repetition:

- "one extra syllable" vs. "no extra syllable"
- clapping the syllables: worked = 1, wanted = 2
- listening pairs: worked/wanted, called/needed, helped/decided
- mouth practice with simple groups: helped, worked, asked; called, cleaned, played; wanted, needed, decided
- color-coding the three endings instead of explaining the voicing rule first
- using sentence rhythm: "I worked yesterday" vs. "I wanted help"
- having students repeat common phrases until they sound familiar

### Daily Routine Ideas

- quick listen-and-repeat at the start of class
- sort 3-6 verbs by `-ed` sound
- add the 3 verbs of the week to the `-ed` chart when possible
- use `-ed` endings during timeline tense practice
- include one `-ed` pronunciation question on weekly quizzes
- have students say a past-tense sentence to a partner
- correct `-ed` endings during speaking stations without stopping the whole conversation
