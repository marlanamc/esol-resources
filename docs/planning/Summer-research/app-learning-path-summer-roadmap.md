# App Learning Path Summer Roadmap

## Summary

This roadmap turns the next-year class improvement goals into a concrete summer product plan for the app.

The priority is enrolled class students. The app should support a teacher-curated weekly path where students can open the app and know exactly what to do next. The goal is to reduce decision fatigue for working adult learners and respect their limited study time.

The core shift:

> Do this next.

Not:

> Pick something from a category menu and figure out what matters.

Students should focus on the language skills, not on deciding what to select.

## Current App Findings

The current app already has many useful pieces, but the student experience is still too browse-oriented.

### What Is Already Working

Compared with previous years, this app is already a major success.

Students are doing more work outside of class than they did with Google Classroom or previous systems. The LMS changed the outside-of-class learning pattern in a real way. The summer goal is not to replace a failed system. The goal is to build on the first system that actually got students reviewing material outside class.

The current data should be read through that lens:

- students are logging in
- students are completing quizzes
- students are opening grammar, games, vocab, and pronunciation activities
- students are doing app work outside of class in a way that did not happen before
- accountability and due dates appear to matter

The next version should protect what worked while reducing friction.

### What Exists Now

- The student dashboard groups assigned work by category: grammar, vocabulary, quizzes, and games.
- Mobile shows Daily Vocab, Resume, then a long task list.
- Students can browse all activities by category.
- Assignments already support instructions, due dates, featured status, and progress.
- The independent learner dashboard already has a `LearningPathRoadmap`.
- The app already has strong activity types: grammar guides, vocabulary, quizzes, games, speaking activities, pronunciation activities, and writing.

### Current Problem

The pieces are good, but the app does not yet clearly tell class students:

- what to do first
- why it matters
- how the activity connects to class
- how the week fits together
- when the work is due

Students see many choices at once. For busy working students, this creates friction. The app should remove that barrier.

The data also suggests that optional habits should not be the main strategy for student practice. If vocabulary matters, it probably needs to be part of the teacher-curated weekly path with a clear due date and class follow-up. Daily Vocab Review may still be valuable as extra practice, review, or personal progress support.

## North Star

The class app should become a guided weekly learning path.

### Community Class First

This is a community class first. English is the tool; real-life confidence is the goal.

Students should build power as:

- consumers who can compare prices, read bills, understand fees, question offers, and avoid scams
- patients and caregivers who can read medicine labels, understand insurance cards, check drug prices, prepare questions, and advocate in health situations
- workers who can understand schedules, pay stubs, policies, complaints, workplace rights, and professional communication
- community members who can use public services, understand local information, participate in community life, and possibly become more confident voters
- readers and thinkers who can interpret charts, notices, forms, data, videos, social media claims, and persuasive arguments

Adult-skill lesson ideas:

- Tylenol vs. Advil
- healthy cooking oils
- fiber + carbohydrates + blood sugar
- medicine labels, insurance cards, dosage charts, nutrition labels
- health claims, product claims, and social media advice

Boundary: the app should not replace doctors, pharmacists, dietitians, lawyers, or financial advisors. It should help students understand information and ask better questions.

The promise should be simple:

> You will leave class more equipped to handle real life in English.

That means grammar, vocabulary, quizzes, games, writing, speaking, and homework should all connect back to real situations. A task is worth assigning when it helps students do something meaningful outside the classroom.

### App As Reference Library

Students already go back to the app to look things up. Treat this as a feature, not an accident.

Reference content to build:

- medicine name charts
- insurance card diagrams
- phone-call scripts
- complaint letter/email templates
- workplace rights vocabulary
- housing repair phrases
- healthy shopping or nutrition label examples
- "questions to ask" checklists for pharmacists, doctors, landlords, employers, schools, and agencies

Design rule: if students saw it in class, they should be able to find it again quickly.

### Product Positioning

This app is not trying to be Duolingo.

Apps like Duolingo can be very helpful for true beginners with little or no prior knowledge. This app is for a different problem: the Level 3 plateau.

The class includes students across roughly NRS high Level 2 through Level 5. ESOL Level 3 is a broad bag of learners, but the goal is that by the end of the year, all students are at least around NRS Level 4.

Many students make rapid progress in years 1-2. By Level 3, they often know a lot more English, but that can create a new problem:

- they overthink
- anxiety increases
- confidence drops
- they know rules but hesitate to use them
- they second-guess simple sentences
- progress can feel slower, even when they are learning more complex language

The app should help students move through that plateau. It should give enough structure to reduce anxiety, but not so much rule-heavy explanation that students freeze.

This means activities need flexible entry points and challenge levels. The same monthly path should support students who still need high Level 2 scaffolding while also pushing stronger students toward Level 4/5 communication.

The app should help students think:

> I know what to do next, I know why it matters, and I can practice this until it feels usable.

### Product Principle

Every assigned task should have a real purpose.

No busywork. No activities assigned just to assign something. If students are asked to spend time on the app, the task should clearly support class, work, home, community life, civic participation, consumer confidence, health literacy, or language confidence.

### Leveling Principle

Each recurring activity format should include support and stretch.

- **Support:** sentence frames, word banks, short examples, partner models, visuals
- **Core:** the expected Level 3 task most students should complete
- **Stretch:** longer answers, follow-up questions, more precise grammar, leadership/share-out roles

The goal is not to build separate classes inside the app. The goal is to make one activity usable for a wide Level 3 class while still moving everyone toward at least NRS Level 4.

### Student Experience Goal

When a student opens the app, they should see:

1. what to do next
2. why it matters
3. when it is due
4. how long it should take
5. what they have already completed

Browsing should still exist, but it should be secondary.

### Skill Chains / Practice Paths

Use Skill Chains to make related activities feel like one purposeful path:

```txt
Micro Lesson -> Preset Game -> Pronunciation Lab -> Use It
```

This should launch existing games and labs in assigned/preset mode so students do not need to choose settings. The dashboard should show the chain as one card with the next incomplete step.

- [Skill Chains / Practice Paths Plan](skill-chains-practice-paths-plan.md)

## Target Weekly Path Model

Each week should become a small guided path connected to the real-world topic of the week.

The monthly unit topics and essential questions should become the curriculum backbone for the app. Grammar guides, microlearning modules, games, vocabulary, speaking tasks, homework, and quizzes should all point back to the month’s essential question.

### Monthly Unit Backbone

| Month | Unit | Topic | Essential Question |
|---|---:|---|---|
| September | 1 | Getting to Know You - Personal Daily Life | How will learners become familiar with overall school and individual classroom procedures as well as each other? |
| October | 2 | Daily Life in the Community | How does staying connected to your community and family play an important role in our lives? |
| November | 3 | Community Participation | Where are communities found? Where do they exist and how do you interact with them? |
| December | 4 | Consumer Smarts | How do we know the best way to spend our money? |
| January | 5 | Housing | How does learning about my community’s housing issues and resources improve my own housing? |
| February | 6 | Workforce Preparation | How will improving my communication skills improve my career opportunities? |
| March | 7 | Career Awareness | How does understanding our rights help us advocate for ourselves in the workplace? |

| May | 9 | Holistic Wellness | How does health impact and affect different areas of my life, including home, work, and community? |
| June | 10 | Future Academic Goals | How does becoming a lifelong learner improve my life? |

### Monthly Design Rule

Every weekly path should answer:

- Which monthly essential question does this support?
- What real-life task helps students answer that question?
- What language skills do students need for that task?
- Which micro lesson, game, vocab set, and quiz item reinforce that task?

### Default Weekly Path

| Step | Activity Type | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Topic opener | Introduce the real-world situation for the week |
| 2 | Micro grammar module | Teach one useful grammar move in context |
| 3 | Game or practice round | Make the grammar active and repeatable |
| 4 | Vocabulary task | Practice 6 required weekly words |
| 5 | Verb and pronunciation task | Practice 3 verbs in **v1 / v1-3rd / v1-ing / v2 / v3** (from September Week 3) plus **monthly Minimal Pair Lab** contrast and `-ed` endings when relevant |
| 6 | Speaking or homework task | Prepare students to use the language in class or real life |
| 7 | Weekly quiz/checkpoint | Check the language and topic understanding |

This path should be flexible, but the default shape gives students and teachers a predictable rhythm.

### Student-Facing Framing

Each item should answer:

- **Why this matters:** what this helps you do
- **Do this:** the exact task
- **Due:** the deadline
- **Time:** estimated minutes

Example:

> **Why this matters:** You will practice asking a pharmacy about medication price and insurance coverage.
>
> **Do this:** Complete the short medicine label module and answer the 5 practice questions.
>
> **Due:** Thursday before class.
>
> **Time:** 8 minutes.

## Rework Areas

### 1. Grammar Guides

Current issue: many grammar guides are too long and can feel like 15-page packets with long mini-quizzes.

Summer direction:

- Convert long guides into microlearning paths.
- Keep rules, but make them short and useful.
- Use real-world contexts first.

Target module shape:

| Part | Goal |
|---|---|
| Learn | one clear idea |
| See it | one real-world example |
| Try it | 3-5 quick practice questions |

### 2. Case Studies And Video

Current issue: case studies were a strong activity this year, but they are not yet treated as a repeatable unit format inside the app plan.

Summer direction:

- Make case studies a recurring unit activity, either every unit or every other unit.
- Pair each case study with a short video, YouTube clip, reel, TikTok, or news explainer when appropriate.
- Use case studies to connect the monthly essential question to real current events.
- Keep the structure predictable so students know how to work through them.

Existing example: `Case Studies – Current Events (1).pdf`.

The current case study format is strong:

| Part | Purpose |
|---|---|
| key vocabulary | pre-teach essential words |
| read together | shared input around a real-world issue |
| quick check | confirm basic comprehension |
| talk it over | support opinion, comparison, and discussion |
| your turn | students write questions they actually want to ask |
| share out | students summarize and lead discussion |

Example topics from the current PDF:

- driverless taxis, technology, and work
- digital nomads, housing, and displacement
- tariffs, prices, and consumer choices
- AI, health decisions, and risk

### Case Study + Video Pattern

Each case study could become a class/app path:

1. preview 6 key words
2. watch a short video or clip
3. read the case study
4. answer quick-check questions
5. discuss opinion questions
6. write two questions for classmates
7. connect to the monthly essential question

Short-form video could be useful because students already encounter information through reels, TikToks, YouTube shorts, and quick explainers. The goal is not to make class less serious; it is to teach students how to process real information, talk about it, and connect it to their lives.

### Video Selection Rules

- Keep videos short.
- Prefer clear speech, captions, and strong visual context.
- Avoid videos that require too much background knowledge.
- Use videos to support discussion, not replace reading.
- Choose videos connected to the monthly unit topic.
- Add a quick purpose statement: "Watch for ___".
- Avoid sending students into an algorithm feed during class if possible.
- For health misinformation or vaccine-related videos, frame the task as claim evaluation, not debate-for-entertainment.
- Pair health-claim analysis with trusted sources such as CDC, FDA, HHS, WHO, Mass.gov, or a local clinic resource.

### Claim Evaluation Activity

Short-form videos can be useful for media literacy. For example, during the Health unit, students could watch a short TikTok or reel that is critical of vaccines and identify the holes in the argument.

The goal is not to mock people or force a political debate. The goal is to practice:

- identifying the main claim
- separating emotion from evidence
- checking the source
- noticing missing numbers or context
- asking what evidence would prove or disprove the claim
- comparing the claim to trusted public health information

Possible student questions:

- What is the person claiming?
- What evidence do they give?
- Is the evidence a story, a number, a study, or an expert source?
- What information is missing?
- Are they trying to scare, inform, sell, or persuade?
- What trusted source could we check?
- What would you ask before believing this?

Useful reference sources:

- [CDC Vaccine Safety Systems](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccine-safety-systems/index.html)
- [HHS Vaccine Safety](https://www.hhs.gov/immunization/basics/safety/index.html)
- [CDC VAERS explanation](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccine-safety-systems/vaers/)
- [WHO: Disinformation and public health](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/disinformation-and-public-health)

### 3. Games

Current issue: games can appear as separate optional extras instead of being clearly connected to the week's learning.

Summer direction:

- Treat games as assigned practice rounds.
- Pair each game with a specific skill.
- Use games after micro lessons so students apply the language immediately.
- Label games by purpose, not only by title.
- Build reusable game formats that can be adapted for every monthly unit.

Example:

- Micro lesson: present perfect for work history
- Game round: timeline tense practice
- Speaking task: "Have you ever...?" interview

### Reusable Unit Game Formats

There is already something strong happening with three game formats:

- **Spin Wheel** - low-pressure prompts, emotions, opinions, sentence starters, or unit vocabulary.
- **Café Catch-Up** - conversation-card discussion decks for real-life topics.
- **Group Trivia** - teacher-led review and retrieval practice.
- **Dice Conversation Boards** - print-friendly partner/group speaking games with repeated question frames.
- **Conversation Board Games** - printable board-game paths with colored spaces and matching task/role-play cards.

These should become repeatable unit templates, not one-off activities.

| Format | Best Use | Unit Adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Spin Wheel | fast speaking, warm-ups, emotional language, sentence frames, vocabulary retrieval | one wheel per unit with prompts tied to the monthly topic |
| Café Catch-Up | longer partner/small-group conversation | one deck per unit with real-life discussion prompts |
| Group Trivia | whole-class review, quiz prep, topic knowledge, grammar retrieval | one trivia set per unit or per major checkpoint |
| Dice Conversation Boards | station work, partner repetition, question/answer frames, grammar in conversation | one printable board per unit, optionally mirrored in the app |
| Conversation Board Games | longer station activity, role-play cards, task cards, movement/turn-taking | one board per unit with color-coded card categories |

Existing examples:

- [Future Simple/Cont Dice Game](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1GFQ1npkavXfDC2ZZ6pynN4lctedpyz0ePqr81_cVoPw/edit?gid=0#gid=0)

- Health Game cards: four decks with six cards each.
  - Prevention & Healthy Habits

The Health Game cards are a strong model because each deck has a clear speaking purpose:

| Deck | Speaking Function |
|---|---|

### Unit Game Pattern

Each monthly unit could include:

1. one Spin Wheel for quick repeated speaking
2. one Café Catch-Up deck for deeper conversation
3. one Group Trivia game for review and retrieval
4. one Dice Conversation Board for station-based speaking
5. one Conversation Board Game for a longer monthly review/speaking station

This gives every unit a familiar rhythm while the content changes. Students do not need to learn a new interface every time. They can focus on the language and topic.

### Printout vs App Decision

The dice boards may be worth keeping as printouts because students like the physical dice and the station format. They do not need to become fully digital if the paper version is working.

Summer decision:

- keep monthly dice boards as print station materials
- create app companion versions for extra practice
- or use both: paper in class, app version for homework/review

The likely best first version is **both**:

- printouts for Tuesday stations
- app companion for students who miss class or want extra practice
- monthly archive so the boards can be reused and revised each year

Conversation board games may be best kept as physical classroom materials first because the movement, dice, and colored cards create real conversation energy. The app could support them by storing the monthly card decks, letting absent students practice prompts, or giving the teacher a printable/exportable card set.

### 4. Texts, Dialogues, Listening, And Video

Current issue: the plan has strong speaking/games/reading pieces, but it needs a more explicit routine for short texts, short dialogues, listening, and video.

Summer direction:

- Add short dialogues as a regular microlearning format.
- Add text-message and real-life short text practice.
- Add structured listening/video tasks, not just "watch this."
- Use short-form videos when they support the unit topic and discussion.
- Make listening and reading lead into speaking or writing.

### Short Dialogue Pattern

Dialogues should be short, practical, and reusable in speaking stations.

Examples:

- calling a doctor's office
- asking a pharmacist about a medicine price
- texting a landlord about a repair
- asking a supervisor about a schedule
- explaining a missing paycheck
- asking an advisor for help

Each dialogue can become:

1. listen or read
2. notice useful phrases
3. practice with a partner
4. change details
5. perform or record/self-check

### Text And Message Pattern

Short texts are important because adult learners often need English for messages, forms, and everyday written communication.

Examples:

- text to a landlord
- message to a boss
- email to a teacher/advisor
- note to a doctor's office
- question for health insurance
- complaint description
- follow-up after a conversation

The app should support practical writing, not essay writing as the default.

### Listening/Video Pattern

Each listening or video activity should have accountability:

1. watch/listen once for the main idea
2. watch/listen again for details
3. answer 2-3 quick checks
4. discuss or write a response
5. connect it to the unit essential question

This matters especially if using YouTube, reels, or TikToks. The video should not be passive entertainment; it should become language input for a real task.

### 6. Error Correction And Notice-The-Difference

Current issue: Level 3 students can communicate, but persistent errors and overthinking can both increase. They need regular, low-stakes practice noticing and fixing language.

Summer direction:

- Add weekly "fix it" or "which sounds better?" micro tasks.
- Use real student-like errors without shaming anyone.
- Focus on one small pattern at a time.
- Pair correction with speaking or writing reuse.
- Start with **guided pattern discovery** before correction drills — students notice the pattern first, then fix it.

Examples:

- choose the better sentence
- fix one mistake
- compare two versions of a text
- notice missing `-ed`
- choose the more polite request
- correct word order in a question
- discover verb + infinitive: `I plan to move` / `We decided to call` / `She wants to ask` → then fix `I plan moving`

### Pattern Discovery Task Template

Use this reusable format for class warm-ups and app micro tasks:

1. **Show** — 6–8 real sentences from one adult-life topic (work, housing, health, school).
2. **Ask** — What is the same after these verbs? What word comes next?
3. **Name** — one-line rule (e.g., "Verbs like plan, decide, want, and need take **to + verb**.").
4. **Try** — 3–5 "which sounds better?" or fill-in choices.
5. **Say** — one speaking prompt that requires the pattern ("What do you plan to do about…?").

**First pattern bank:** verb + infinitive (`plan`, `decide`, `want`, `need`, `hope`, `try`).

**Later patterns:** `-ed` endings, question word order, tense choice on a timeline, articles after count/non-count nouns, polite requests.

**App flow:** learn phrase → notice pattern → try → say it → check.

**Class flow:** Tuesday notice activity → grammar station practice → Thursday speaking retrieval.

### 7. Reflection And Confidence Tracking

Current issue: quiz scores do not fully capture the Level 3 plateau, confidence, or anxiety.

Summer direction:

- Add light reflection and confidence check-ins.
- Keep them short and adult.
- Use them to help students see progress.
- Use them to guide teacher decisions.
- Add a short monthly student survey so the class can evaluate what is actually helping.

Possible prompts:

- I can talk about this topic: not yet / a little / yes
- One phrase I want to remember is...
- This week I used English to...
- The most useful thing this week was...
- I still feel confused about...

### Monthly Survey Pattern

Once a month, students should complete a short survey. It should take 3-5 minutes.

The survey should help answer:

- Did students know what to do in the app?
- Which activity helped most?
- Which activity felt like a waste of time?
- Did homework feel useful?
- Did students feel more confident speaking about the monthly topic?
- Which format should be repeated next month?

Possible monthly survey questions:

1. This month, I understood what to do in the app.
2. The homework helped me prepare for class.
3. The activities helped me speak more confidently.
4. The most useful activity this month was...
5. One activity I did not find useful was...
6. I want more practice with...
7. I feel more confident talking about this unit topic: not yet / a little / yes.

Survey results should become part of the planning process, not extra paperwork.

- Add small real-world math and data literacy tasks.
- Use charts, prices, percentages, bills, schedules, health numbers, and workplace data.
- Teach students to ask good questions about data.
- Connect this to consumer smarts, housing, work, health, wellness, and current events.
- Include science literacy when it appears naturally, especially in health and wellness units.

The goal is not to turn ESOL into a math class. The goal is to help students understand the numbers and claims they meet in adult life.

### Data Questions Students Should Practice

- Who made this chart?
- What is the source?
- What number is missing?
- Is this a percent or a total number?
- What changed over time?
- Who benefits if I believe this?
- Is this claim about all people or only some people?
- Is the graph trying to persuade me?
- What question should I ask before trusting this?

### Possible Activities

- understand rent increase percentages
- compare health statistics
- read a medicine dosage chart
- evaluate a politician's claim
