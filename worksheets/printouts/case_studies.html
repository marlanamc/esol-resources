The Driverless Taxi
Technology & Work · United States, 2026
Case Study
Key Vocabulary
self-driving — a car that uses computers to drive, with no person at the wheel
expand — to move into new cities or areas
Senate hearing — an official government meeting where leaders ask questions
overseas — in a different country
admitted — said something true that was difficult or embarrassing
benefit — to get something good from a change or situation
Read Together

Waymo is a company owned by Google. It makes self-driving taxis — cars with no human driver inside. You order one on an app, the same way you would call an Uber or Lyft. Right now, Waymo runs in Phoenix, Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Atlanta. It plans to expand to Boston and more cities soon.

Waymo says its cars are much safer than human drivers. The company's data shows 90% fewer serious injuries compared to regular cars. Researchers who studied the numbers mostly agree. Waymo has now driven more than 170 million miles with no person behind the wheel.

But in February 2026, a U.S. Senate hearing brought out a surprising fact. Waymo's leaders admitted that about half of the workers who help guide the cars from a distance are based in the Philippines. These workers don't actually drive the car — they give advice when the car gets confused, like a backup helper. The car's computer stays in control. Still, some senators were angry. One said: "It's one thing when a taxi is replaced by Uber. It's another when the jobs go completely overseas."

This raises a bigger question: who benefits when technology replaces old jobs? Taxi drivers, truck drivers, and delivery workers across the country are paying close attention. Some experts say self-driving cars will create new types of jobs. Others say those new jobs will pay less and go to workers in other countries. At the same time, the Filipino workers are grateful — the pay is much better than most jobs available to them locally.

The New Neighbors
Housing & Displacement · Medellín, Colombia
Case Study
Key Vocabulary
digital nomad — a person who works online and lives in different countries
gentrification — when wealthier people move in and prices rise for everyone
displacement — when people are pushed out of their neighborhood
short-term rental — an apartment rented for days or weeks, like on Airbnb
remote work — working from home or a café instead of an office
afford — to have enough money to pay for something
Read Together

Since the COVID-19 pandemic, thousands of Americans and Europeans have moved to Medellín, Colombia — not as tourists, but to live and work from their laptops. These people are called digital nomads. They earn American or European salaries but pay Colombian prices. For them, Medellín had everything: warm weather, good food, fast internet, and affordable rent. But that rent is not affordable anymore.

The problem is simple: when people with higher salaries move into a neighborhood, prices go up for everyone. This is called gentrification. In popular areas like El Poblado and Laureles, rents have gone up 80% in just a few years. Colombian families who lived there for decades can no longer afford to stay. They are being pushed out of their own neighborhoods.

Many digital nomads say they love Medellín and its culture. Some learn Spanish, volunteer, and try to give back. But local residents are frustrated. One said: "The rent prices are increasing because foreigners can pay that. Sometimes you see an apartment and think, this isn't worth that much." Local businesses — bakeries, repair shops, family restaurants — are closing and being replaced by co-working spaces and international cafés.

The Colombian government is now looking at new laws to control rents and limit short-term rentals. Cities like Lisbon and Barcelona have already passed laws like this. This is not just a Medellín problem — it is happening everywhere that remote workers move to cheaper cities.

Why Is Everything So Expensive?
Trade & Prices · United States, 2026
Case Study
Key Vocabulary
tariff — a tax added to products that come from other countries
imported — brought into the country from somewhere else
absorb (a cost) — to pay an extra cost yourself instead of charging the customer
income — the money you earn from your job
impact — the real effect that something has on people's lives
debate — a discussion where people disagree and share different views
Read Together

Have you noticed your grocery bill getting bigger? There is a real reason. In 2025 and 2026, the U.S. government added new taxes on products coming from other countries. These taxes are called tariffs. The idea was to protect American businesses — if foreign products cost more, maybe people will buy American products instead.

But the problem is that the U.S. cannot grow or make everything it needs. About 80% of the seafood Americans eat comes from other countries. So does 80% of the coffee, 59% of fresh fruit, and almost all bananas and chocolate. When the government adds a tariff, the companies that bring these products in pay more — and then they charge you more at the store.

Experts say the full impact of these tariffs is hitting now, in 2026. Food prices are expected to go up nearly 3% just because of tariffs. Coffee from Brazil now has a 50% tariff. Olive oil, seafood, cheese, and tropical fruits are all getting more expensive. Smaller grocery stores, which often serve immigrant communities, are raising prices faster than big chains like Walmart, because they don't have extra money to absorb the costs.

Some people support the tariffs. They say America needs to make its own products and stop depending on other countries. But others say tariffs hurt the poorest families the most — families who already spend a large part of their income on food. The debate about tariffs is really a question: who pays the price?

Should You Ask AI About Your Health?
Technology & Health · United States, 2026
Case Study
Key Vocabulary
survey — a study that asks many people the same questions
language barrier — when you can't get help because you don't speak the language well
symptoms — signs that something is wrong with your body (pain, fever, rash)
inaccurate — wrong; not correct
hallucination — (AI) when the computer makes up an answer that sounds true but is not
pharmacist — the person at the pharmacy who gives you your medicine
Read Together

You have a bad headache or a rash that won't go away. Do you go to the doctor — or do you ask ChatGPT? More and more Americans are using AI instead of talking to a doctor. A 2026 survey found that 51% of adults in the U.S. used AI to make an important health decision without calling a doctor or nurse first.

It's easy to see why. AI is free. It's always available. And it answers fast. For immigrant families who face language barriers, expensive doctor visits, or no health insurance, AI can feel like the only option. You can describe your symptoms in any language and get an answer in seconds.

But doctors are worried. AI sometimes gives wrong answers — and it sounds very sure of itself when it does. A doctor at Ohio State University said: "We know that 2% of the time, AI is going to be inaccurate. I would be really concerned about a patient who is following AI." Two percent doesn't sound like a lot. But millions of people use AI every day. That means thousands of people could be getting bad advice.

In other parts of the world, the story is different. In rural Kenya, there are almost no doctors. AI-powered apps help pharmacists figure out what is wrong with patients and what medicine to give them. The same technology that worries doctors in the U.S. is saving lives in places that don't have enough doctors. So the real question is: when is AI helpful for your health, and when is it dangerous?
