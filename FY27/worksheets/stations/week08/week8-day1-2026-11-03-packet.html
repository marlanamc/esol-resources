STATION 3: READElection Night · with your group
Page 6
Words to know
polling place = where you go to vote
poll worker = a helper at the polling place
registered = your name is on the list of voters
candidate = a person who wants the job
ballot = the paper you vote on
Congress = leaders who make national laws

Today is Election Day in Massachusetts. People vote for governor, for Congress, and for many other leaders. Most polling places open at 7 a.m. The polls close at 8 p.m. If you are a U.S. citizen, 18 or older, and registered, you can vote.

Ana became a citizen this year, so this is her first election. She is excited and a little nervous. In the morning, she checks her polling place online. It is in an elementary school near her apartment. “If I leave work at 5, I will vote before class,” she tells her friend Samir.

At 5:30, Ana walks into the school gym. The line is short. A poll worker asks for her name and address. He finds her name on the list and gives her a paper ballot. Ana goes to a small table and fills in the circles with a pen. Then she puts her ballot into the machine. The poll worker gives her a sticker: “I Voted.”

Not everyone votes on Election Day. Ana’s neighbor, Mr. Lopez, is 80 years old. He doesn’t like to stand in long lines, so he voted by mail last month. “If you vote by mail, you don’t wait in line,” he says.

Samir is not a citizen yet, so he cannot vote. But leaders work for everyone in their city, not only voters. Last spring, the streetlight on Samir’s street broke. If a streetlight breaks, you contact the city. Samir called his city councilor’s office. They responded in two days, and the city fixed the light.

Different leaders have different jobs. If you have a problem on your street, you contact your city or town. If you have a question about a state law, you contact your state representative. If you have a question about a national law, you contact your members of Congress.

Samir has a plan too. His citizenship interview is in the spring. “If I pass my test, I will vote in the next election,” he says. Ana smiles. “If you need help with the questions, I will study with you.”

Tonight, Ana will watch the news after class. “If my candidate wins, I will celebrate with my family,” she says. “If my candidate loses, I will be sad. But I will vote again next time.” Some results come tonight. Others come in a few days. The winners start their new jobs in January.
