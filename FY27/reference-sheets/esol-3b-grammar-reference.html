01 / Build a Sentence

The jobs words do

Start with the job a word does in this sentence. The same word can do different jobs: I work (verb); my work (noun).

Noun

Names a person, place, thing, or idea. Can be a subject or object.

The student reads a book.

Pronoun

Replaces a noun. Use subject forms before the verb; object forms after a verb or preposition.

She called him. This is for us.

Verb

Shows an action or state. Usually follows the subject in statements.

They study. She is tired. I know.

Article

A / an introduces one countable thing. The identifies a specific one.

I need a pen. Please pass the blue pen.

Adjective

Describes a noun or pronoun; often before a noun or after be / look / feel / seem.

a quiet room; The room is quiet.

Adverb

Describes a verb, adjective, adverb, or a whole sentence. Position depends on its job.

She speaks clearly. It is very cold.

Preposition

Shows a relationship in time, place, or direction. Follow with a noun, pronoun, or -ing form.

at work; with him; before leaving

Conjunction

Connects words, phrases, or clauses. FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.

tea or coffee; I was tired, but I finished.

See each word’s job

The listener knows which one; a specific item or group.

The appointment we discussed is today.

General plural or uncountable nouns.

Books help us learn. Water is important.

Subject + verb + the rest of the idea.
She works at the hospital. / He is a driver. / They feel happy.

01 / Build a Sentence

Adjectives, adverbs & frequency

Adjectives

Describe: Nouns and pronouns

Questions: What kind? Which one? How many?

Examples: a red car; three books; You look happy.

Adverbs

Describe: Verbs, adjectives, other adverbs, whole sentences

Questions: How? When? Where? How often? To what extent?

Examples: She drives carefully. It is very cold. Luckily, we arrived.

Not always -ly Good → well: She is a good driver. She drives well. Fast → fast; hard → hard. Hardly means almost not, not “with effort.”

How often?

always
I always bring my binder.
usually / normally
I usually take the bus. I normally arrive early.
often
We often study together.
sometimes
I sometimes work on Saturdays.
occasionally
We occasionally meet after class.
seldom / rarely
She rarely misses class.
hardly ever
He hardly ever watches TV.
never
I never leave without my keys.

These words show a general frequency, not exact percentages. Always is every time; never is no time.

Word order Before most main verbs: I usually walk. After be: I am usually early. After the first helping verb: I have never been there. Sometimes can also begin or end a sentence.

Prepositions of place

Prepositions of movement

Choose the form for your sentence

Past: She went to work. → Use V2.
Perfect: She has gone to work. → After have / has / had, use V3.

Simple Tenses

Say what happens, happened, or will happen.

Routines & facts • finished past • predictions & decisions

Present Simple

She takes the bus every morning.

Habits, routines, facts, and states. Add -s / -es for he, she, it in affirmative statements.

Predictions, promises, offers, and decisions made as we speak.

03 / Verbs & Tenses

Continuous Tenses

Picture an action in progress.

At that moment, the action is happening.

Present Continuous

I am washing the dishes right now.

An action happening now or around now; a temporary situation. Also a future arrangement with a time expression.

PASTNOWFUTURE

Time clues now, right now, at the moment, this week

Remember I am studying this month. / I am meeting my teacher tomorrow. State verbs such as know, need, and believe usually use simple forms.

Past Continuous

I was cooking when the phone rang.

An action in progress at a past time. Often gives background for a shorter past action.

PASTNOWFUTURE

Time clues at 8 last night, while, when

Remember I was cooking when the phone rang. Cooking was in progress; the call happened during it. Two ongoing actions can both use past continuous.

Future Continuous

I will be working at 8 tomorrow.

An action that will be in progress at a particular future time.

PASTNOWFUTURE

Time clues this time tomorrow, at 8 tomorrow, when you arrive

Remember At 8 tomorrow, I will be working. This describes the activity in progress, not its completion.

03 / Verbs & Tenses

Perfect Tenses

Look back from a point in time.

Connect an earlier action or situation to now, then, or a future point.

Present Perfect

I have finished my homework already.

Past experience, a present result, or a situation that began in the past and continues now.

PASTNOWFUTURE

Time clues ever, never, already, yet, just, since, for

Remember I have lost my keys (I cannot find them now). I lost them yesterday (finished past time). Do not pair present perfect with a finished time such as yesterday.

Past Perfect

She had left before the meeting started.

An action finished before another past action or time.

PASTNOWFUTURE

Time clues already, before, by the time

Remember When I arrived, class had started. Class started first. Do not use past perfect simply because an event happened long ago.

Future Perfect

She will have finished the report by noon.

An action that will be finished by a future time or deadline.

PASTNOWFUTURE

Time clues by Friday, by then, by the time

Remember By Friday, I will have finished the report. After by the time in a future time clause, use present simple: by the time you arrive.

Perfect Continuous Tenses

Focus on how long the activity continues.

Follow an activity up to now, a past point, or a future point.

Present Perfect Continuous

I have been living here for two years.

An activity continuing from the past to now, or recently stopped with a present result. Emphasizes activity or duration.

PASTNOWFUTURE

Time clues for, since, all morning, lately

Remember I have been studying for two hours. I have studied three chapters emphasizes a completed amount. Use I have known her, not have been knowing.

Past Perfect Continuous

They had been playing for hours before dinner.

An activity continuing up to a past time. It may explain a past result.

PASTNOWFUTURE

Time clues for, since, before, when, by the time

Remember I was tired because I had been working all day. The work happened before that past feeling.

Future Perfect Continuous

By June, I will have been working here for a year.

How long an activity will have continued by a future time.

PASTNOWFUTURE

Time clues for + duration, by then, by the time

Remember By June, I will have been studying here for a year. The emphasis is how long; the activity may continue.

Two actions: background & order

A sentence can connect two actions. Decide whether one action was in progress, one happened earlier, or both continued together.

RelationshipTense pairingExample
An action during anotherpast continuous + past simpleI was studying when the power went out.
Two ongoing actionspast continuous + past continuousWhile I was cooking, he was washing the dishes.
An earlier completed actionpast perfect + past simpleShe had left before I arrived.
Duration before a past eventpast perfect continuous + past simpleThey had been waiting for an hour when the bus came.

Read the order, not just the order of words

1She had left.EARLIER
→
2I arrived.LATER

When I arrived, she had left.
First: she left. Then: I arrived.
She had left when I arrived describes the same order.

When / while While often introduces an ongoing action. When can introduce a point in time. Neither word automatically chooses the tense; the meaning does.
An interruption is only one possibility An earlier completed event is not an interruption. Avoid a blanket rule such as “drop one tense back.”

Future time clauses

After when, before, after, until, as soon as, and by the time, use a present form for a future time clause.

Main ideaTime clauseComplete example
will + V1when + present simpleI will call when I arrive.
will be + V-ingwhen + present simpleI will be working when you arrive.
will have + V3by the time + present simpleWe will have finished by the time the guests arrive.
will have been + V-ingwhen + present simpleWhen June comes, I will have been studying here for a year.

Choose the meaning

START

I will cook when you arrive.

Cooking will start then.

IN PROGRESS

I will be cooking when you arrive.

Cooking will be in progress.

FINISHED

I will have cooked by the time you arrive.

Cooking will be finished.

Present perfect is also possible Use it to emphasize completion: I will call after I have finished work. Present simple is also possible: after I finish work.
Time clause or question? I will call when she arrives is a time clause. I do not know when she will arrive is an indirect question, so will is possible.

Comma guide

Time clause first: When I arrive, I will call.
Main clause first: I will call when I arrive.

Connect actions in time

Punctuation that helps your reader

Two complete ideas + and / but / so: use a comma before the connector.
I called, but no one answered.

However / therefore / in addition / for example: a new sentence is an easy choice. Put a comma after the opening connector.
The office was closed. However, I could leave a message.

Keep the connection complete Because / since / although + subject + verb needs a main clause. If it comes first, follow it with a comma: Because I was sick, I stayed home. Use such as + noun examples, not a new sentence.

Conditionals: condition & result

The if clause gives a condition. The main clause gives its result. Choose the pattern for the meaning, not only the time word.

0

Zero: general truth or routine

CONDITIONif + present simple
↓
RESULTpresent simple

If I work late, I take the bus.

1

First: real future possibility

CONDITIONif + present simple
↓
RESULTwill + V1

If it rains tomorrow, I will stay home.

2

Second: imagined now or future

CONDITIONif + past simple
↓
RESULTwould + V1

If I had more time, I would cook more.

3

Third: imagined past

CONDITIONif + past perfect
↓
RESULTwould have + V3

If I had known, I would have helped.

Change the clause order, keep the meaning

If it rains, I will stay home. / I will stay home if it rains.
Use a comma when the if clause comes first.

Meaning matters The second conditional uses a past form for an imagined present or future: If I had a car, I would drive. It does not necessarily describe past time.
Useful variations If I were you, I would ask. Could and might can replace would when the meaning is ability or possibility. A first-conditional result can give advice or a command: If you need help, ask.
Avoid the common mix-up For the usual first conditional, use If it rains, not “If it will rain.” For the third, use would have + V3, not “would + present perfect.”

Modals: meaning & form

Core modals use modal + V1: she can drive, not “can drives” or “can to drive.” Negatives add not; questions put the modal first.

can

Ability; permission; requests

“I can swim. Can I sit here?”

could

Past general ability; possibility; polite requests

“I could swim at six. Could you help?”

may

Possibility; formal permission

“It may rain. May I come in?”

might

Possibility

“The bus might be late.”

must

Strong obligation; strong deduction

“You must sign here. She must be tired.”

should / ought to

TensePassive formulaActive → passive
Present simpleam / is / are + V3The teacher checks it. → It is checked.
Past simplewas / were + V3They repaired it. → It was repaired.
Present continuousam / is / are + being + V3They are moving it. → It is being moved.
Past continuouswas / were + being + V3They were moving it. → It was being moved.
Present perfecthave / has + been + V3They have finished it. → It has been finished.
Past perfecthad + been + V3They had found it. → It had been found.

Modalmodal + be + V3They should clean it. → It should be cleaned.

When is passive useful?

The doer is unknown: My bike was stolen.
The result matters: The bridge was repaired.
The doer is obvious: He was arrested.
A formal or less direct statement: A mistake was made.

Build it carefully The old object becomes the new subject. Match be to the new subject: The rooms are cleaned. Keep V3. Add by + doer only when useful.
Questions and negatives Was it delivered? / It was not delivered. Change the first helping verb. Verbs without an object, such as arrive, do not normally form a passive.

Reported speech: tell what someone said

Direct speech gives exact words. Reported speech gives the message. Adjust pronouns to show who is speaking and who is being discussed.

Common shift after said / toldDirect speechReported speech

past → past perfect“I moved in 2020.”He said he had moved in 2020.
present perfect → past perfect“I have finished.”He said he had finished.
will → would“I will call.”She said she would call.

YOUR REPORTShe said she was tired.

Said or told?

She said (that) she was tired.
She told me (that) she was tired.
Use an object with told: told me / told us. Say said to me, not “said me.”

Backshift is not automatic When reporting later with a past reporting verb, a tense shift is common. If the fact is still true, keeping the original tense can be appropriate: She said she lives in Boston. With says, often keep the tense: She says she is tired.
Keep the speaker’s meaning Do not change names, dates, or facts mechanically. “I graduated in 1975” still refers to 1975 when reported.
